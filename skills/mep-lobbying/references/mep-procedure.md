## 1. How a session flows
1. **Preparation:** research the topic from the study guide and official EU sources and form
   your own position. Many MEP sessions say **do not arrive with a finished resolution**,
   because the committee writes it **together**. Arrive with research, a position and ideas.
2. **Committee work:** brainstorm, then draft **introductory clauses (ICs)** and **operative
   clauses (OCs)**, then choose speakers (opening speech, closing speech, amendment
   responses).
3. **Lobbying:** delegates read other committees' resolutions, look for weaknesses and
   prepare amendments and questions.
4. **General Assembly (GA):** each committee's resolution is debated and voted on by
   delegations.

## 2. Resolution format (default template)

```
Committee on [name] ([ACRONYM]) submits the following resolution to the Model European
Parliament about [topic].

The Model European Parliament,

A. [Introductory phrase in italics] [fact or concern, ideally with source and date],

B. [Introductory phrase] ...,

C. ...;

1. [Operative verb in bold] [actor] to [specific action] [by when / how funded];

2. [Operative verb] ...:
   a) ...,
   b) ...;

3. ... .
```

Conventions:
- ICs are lettered (A, B, C...); OCs are numbered (1, 2, 3...); sub-clauses use a), b), c).
- ICs describe the problem and the context and can cite sources with numbers and dates.
  They do **not** propose action.
- Each OC proposes **one specific action** by **one clearly named actor**.
- Punctuation: ICs end with commas; OCs end with semicolons; the final OC ends with a full
  stop. The whole resolution is grammatically one sentence.
- Aim for more OCs than ICs. Solutions matter more than background.
- Typical size: 5–9 ICs and 7–12 OCs. Follow any limit the Presidency sets.

## 3. Introductory-clause phrase bank
Alarmed by · Acknowledging · Aware of · Bearing in mind · Concerned by · Convinced that ·
Deeply concerned about · Deeply regretting · Emphasising · Fully aware of · Having
considered · Keeping in mind · Noting with concern · Noting with satisfaction · Observing ·
Realising · Recalling · Recognising · Reaffirming · Seeking · Stressing · Taking into
account · Taking note of · Welcoming

Patterns that work on any topic:
- *Alarmed by* [a dated, sourced statistic showing the size of the problem] ([source], [date]),
- *Recalling* [an existing EU law, strategy or Parliament resolution, with its date],
- *Welcoming* [a recent Commission proposal or Council decision] of [date],
- *Noting with concern* [the gap between the policy and the result, with a number],
- *Bearing in mind* that [the policy area] is a [exclusive / shared / supporting] competence under Articles 3–6 TFEU,
- *Emphasising* the perspective of young Europeans, who [specific youth-relevant fact],

## 4. Operative-clause phrase bank
Calls upon · Calls on · Urges · Encourages · Recommends · Requests · Invites · Further
invites · Supports · Welcomes · Proposes · Suggests · Emphasises · Reminds · Stresses ·
Endorses · Affirms · Approves · Designates · Expresses its hope · Regrets · Has resolved ·
Further recommends · Strongly encourages · Takes note · Trusts · Commits (for the
Parliament's own actions)

Avoid verbs that imply power the Parliament doesn't have: "orders", "forces", "obliges",
"mandates", "bans" (unless quoting an existing law).

## 5. Amendments and textual changes
- **Amendments** change **operative clauses only**: add, delete or change text. They are
  usually submitted to the Presidency in writing by a deadline, and the Presidency decides
  which ones go to debate.
- Typical format:
  `Amendment to OC 4 submitted by the delegation of Bulgaria: Change "calls on member states to ban..." to "encourages member states to limit..."`
- **Textual changes** are grammar or typo fixes agreed by the whole committee. They cannot
  change meaning.

## 6. General Assembly debate order and timings (default)
1. Textual changes proclaimed by the Presidency (no debate).
2. **Reading** of the resolution by a committee member, verbatim.
3. **Opening speech** by a committee member presenting the resolution: about **3 minutes**.
4. **Amendment(s):** the proposer defends it; questions from the floor; a committee speaker
   responds (about **2 minutes**) and may recommend a vote; then a vote, usually with no
   abstentions.
5. **Open debate:** delegates ask questions; committee members answer and defend the resolution.
6. **Speech against** the resolution by a delegate from the floor: about **3 minutes**.
7. **Closing speech** for the resolution by a committee member, answering the speech against
   and summing up: about **3 minutes**. No further comments.
8. **Voting** (see below).

## 7. Voting (default)
- Each delegation consults (about 1 minute); the head of delegation announces the votes for,
  against and abstaining.
- Committee presidents' votes may be announced separately and not counted.
- A resolution passes if "for" votes exceed "against" votes. Abstentions don't count against it.

## 8. Speaking etiquette and points
- Raise your placard or badge and wait to be recognised. Address the chair as "Madam
  President" or "Mr President" and thank them for the floor.
- Speak in the **third person**: "The delegation of Bulgaria believes...", "Would the
  delegate from Portugal agree that...".
- One placard per delegation in plenary. Never raise it while someone is speaking.
- **Point of personal privilege:** you can't hear, or you're uncomfortable. It may interrupt
  a speaker.
- **Point of order:** procedure has been broken. Not all sessions use it.
- **Point of information / question:** a question to the speaker, when the chair allows it.
- **Formal register:** no personal attacks, calm tone, respect earlier arguments.
- English is the working language. Formal dress is usually expected.

## 9. The one rule people forget
A committee president leads but **does not give opinions or change the resolution's
content**. If you're a delegate, the content is yours. Own it, and make sure you can explain
every clause in your own words.
